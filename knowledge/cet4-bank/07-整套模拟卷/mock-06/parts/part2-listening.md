<summary>Passage One 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

In 1892 a Scottish chemist working in London built a bottle that kept boiling water hot for a day and liquid hydrogen cold for hours, with no fuel and no packing at all. James Dewar, a lecturer at the Royal Institution, needed gases in liquid form while he examined them, so he fitted one thin glass tube inside another, silvered the surfaces that faced each other, and drew the air out of the space between them. With no air left, heat could travel neither by touching nor by moving current, and the bright walls threw back whatever warmth arrived by radiation. Dewar asked for no legal rights over what he had made, and colleagues remembered a man uninterested in money. That silence opened a door. Two Berlin glassblowers who had watched his demonstrations filed papers of their own in 1904 and sold the rights to a factory in Hanover, which shipped a household version under the name Thermos. For six decades that was a brand. In 1963 an American court agreed with a rival maker that the public had long used the word for any such bottle, so it slipped out of protection and into the dictionary, and the German firm kept none of it. The design still holds a place in laboratories: a steel vacuum vessel carries liquid nitrogen, medical samples and vaccines across countries with unreliable power, and every cheap flask on a camping shelf is his, though almost nobody buying it has heard his name.

<summary>Passage Two 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

Before 1963 a letter bound for a small town had to be read by somebody who knew the way there. The United States Post Office Department answered with digits, but not quickly. A national scheme of numbered districts appeared in 1948 and was refused by a committee of the House, on the argument that citizens would resent being marked with figures instead of the name of their town. When the plan finally arrived, on July 1, 1963, it stood five digits wide and carried the name ZIP, from Zone Improvement Plan. The first figure sorts the country into ten groups of states, low in the northeast, high in the far west. The next two point to a sectional center, usually one large building reading the envelopes of a city and its suburbs. The last two name the office that gives the letter to the person it waits for. Numbers let mail ride onto belts and into machines, and the department worked as hard to sell the habit as to build it: singers recorded short tunes for radio, and a drawn character called Miss Zip went out to the schools, so that children taught their parents. In 1983 the code grew a hyphen and four further figures, pinning an address to one block, one tall building, or one sender who ships by the pallet, which is why the string typed online now often runs to nine. Britain had started the same road at Norwich in 1959 and spent fifteen years coding streets across the whole country.

<summary>Passage Three 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

Radar was not the first attempt to catch an aircraft from the ground; for a while the likely answer seemed to be concrete. Between 1916 and the early 1920s experimenters raised curved reflectors along the coasts of southern and eastern England. Some of them were bowls twenty meters across, each shaped to bring faint noise out of the open air together at one point, where a soldier stood with headphones and wrote down what he heard. In calm weather a machine twenty miles out could be picked out above the sea, and two or three stations together could point roughly at where it came from. The method gave way for a reason that had nothing to do with concrete. Motors became smoother and planes grew faster, until what reached the coast was lost in the wash of surf and wind, while a system of sent pulses and waiting echoes found machines many times farther off without an ear in the process. Builders had used the same physics much longer. The theater at Epidaurus, cut into a hillside in the fourth century BCE, seats fourteen thousand on stone steps, and a speaker at the middle of the stage is followed easily nearly to the crown of the slope. In the walk at the top of the dome of St. Paul's Cathedral, a whisper sent along the smooth curve on one side arrives clearly at an ear forty meters away, because such a surface keeps sound from spreading.
