<summary>Passage One 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

For thirty years the distance an average American drives each day has barely changed, while the share of trips taken on foot has fallen to about one in fifty. Researchers in Melbourne decided to test whether the built environment could push that number the other way, and their findings are now changing how several cities approve new housing. The study followed more than two thousand adults for three years, mapping every street within a kilometer of their homes and asking them to record where they actually went on foot. The strongest predictor turned out not to be parks or scenery but something drier: how many crossings a person needed to reach the next block. Districts built on long superblocks, where a shop sits two kilometers away behind a wall of garages, produced almost no walking at all, whatever residents said about their intentions. Small blocks with frequent intersections kept people on their feet, and the effect held after the researchers allowed for income, age and whether a household owned a car. A second result was less obvious: steps gathered in a few repeatable spots, so benches, shade and safe crossings brought outsize benefits where they were placed. Planners now argue that laying pavement beside existing wide roads achieves far less than cutting those roads into shorter pieces.

<summary>Passage Two 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

Ask a student how to prepare for an examination and most will answer with late nights and strong coffee. Ask a neuroscientist and you are likely to hear the opposite. During deep sleep the brain runs through the day's events at high speed, a process researchers call consolidation, and in doing so it carries fragile new information out of a temporary holding area and into permanent storage. In one well-known experiment, volunteers learned a sequence of finger taps and were tested twelve hours later. Half of them slept during that interval and half stayed awake. The rested group improved by roughly ten percent on average, and the gains were still there a week later, while repetition with no sleep in between produced almost nothing. A second finding is stranger, because rest appears to choose. Not everything learned between breakfast and bedtime survives; cues presented around a study session, such as a particular smell or a brief tone, can tip which traces are kept, and anything tagged as emotionally important is pushed to the front of the queue. That helps explain why a student who works four hours and then sleeps often does better than one who works eight and does not. It also explains a cruelty in the same mechanism: an all-nighter damages recall of precisely the material the extra hours were meant to protect.

<summary>Passage Three 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

How does a monarch butterfly, which has never made the journey, find a mountain valley it visited only in its ancestry? The answer, increasingly, is that the route arrives preloaded. Monarchs crossing North America live in a chain of generations: the individuals that travel thousands of kilometers are great-great-grandchildren of those that left the same valleys the previous autumn. Field experiments with raised nets and displaced adults show that the insects combine a sun compass, which needs clear light, with an internal clock, so that they can correct for the sun's travel across the sky. Cloudy days do not stop them, because they also read polarization, a pattern in the light that survives cloud and that human eyes cannot detect. Pigeons turn out to be even less single-minded. They use the Earth's magnetic field, odors carried along familiar routes and possibly low-frequency sound, and when researchers blocked one channel the birds simply relied on the rest. The most surprising navigation belongs to the desert ant, which keeps a tally of its own strides. Scientists glued hair-thin extensions to some animals' legs and shortened others by amputation; the foragers then overshot or fell short of their nests by exactly the amount the alteration predicted. No one has found an insect that uses only one sense to get home.
