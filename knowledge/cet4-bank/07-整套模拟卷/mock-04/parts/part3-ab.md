**The quiet quay**

**A)** Ramon Delgado spent nineteen years hooking containers onto cranes at a pier in Long Beach, and he can name the year the sound changed. "The quay got quiet," he says. "You used to hear men shouting for a move, somebody blowing a whistle. Now there is a hum, and the boxes lift themselves." His terminal can load a vessel holding twelve thousand containers with a dockside crew of about fifty, where the same work once took more than a hundred and thirty men. Nobody was laid off; the company simply stopped hiring and moved the men it kept indoors.

**B)** What happened at that pier is now the plan for the trade. Ports on four continents have committed something near eighty billion dollars to driverless handling since 2019, and analysts put automation at roughly a quarter of global container throughput, up from about one tenth in 2012, with a third expected before 2030. The pattern holds in rich and poor harbors alike: Rotterdam, Singapore, Shanghai's new Lingang berth, a terminal in Egypt, half a dozen Mexican ports chasing the same ships with the same gantries. Money moves first; the argument about what it means arrives years later, from the people standing on the concrete.

**C)** The machines themselves are not new. The first unmanned container berth opened in Europe in 1993, and it very nearly bankrupted its operator, because the equipment of that era could not reliably read the number painted on a steel box. Everything difficult about automation sat in the eyes. Since about 2015, cameras and laser scanners have become cheap enough to fit every crane, and centimeter-accurate positioning signals cost nothing. The steel barely changed; what changed is that a terminal can now see.

**D)** The reason operators are spending is arithmetic rather than ambition. Ships have grown into floating cities: a vessel carrying twenty-four thousand containers has to be in and out of a berth inside about a day, because every hour tied up costs its owner tens of thousands of dollars. Clearing such a ship demands a box every ninety seconds, all night, in the rain, at a pace no gang can hold through shift premiums and coffee breaks. Machines do not price the night differently, and they do not need a platform forty meters up, which is where the safest place for a human being used to be.

**E)** Workers have answered, and so far the answer is a contract. A walkout in 2024 idled every major container harbor on the American east and gulf coasts, and one business federation figured the loss at about five billion dollars a week. It started over overtime rules and a six-day week. After a long negotiation it ended with something rarer: an agreement that the unions' members, not the employers, would decide whether new machines could be brought in, covering most of those ports until 2035. Nobody called it a victory, and everybody kept the language.

**F)** Inside the control rooms, the men who stayed describe something mixed. Delgado now sits six blocks from the water in a room of screens, pushing a lever that walks a crane along the quay while cameras keep a worker's boot out of its path. His back no longer aches and he no longer climbs. He also struggles to name what has gone: "Nobody on the ship waves at me. Nobody needs me to be quick." A training supervisor said the hardest thing to teach a former hookman is trusting a screen for six hours.

**G)** What automation does to the payroll is the most argued number in this industry, and the honest answer is that shape changes faster than total. Since 2015 the gangs on the wharf have shrunk by half or more, while hiring has grown in the buildings around them: remote operators, crane electricians, laser technicians, stowage planners, and a new cohort in the warehouses a kilometer inland where boxes are opened. Port authorities in the Netherlands and in Singapore each report combined employment at or above what it was before their first unmanned berth, and for a young worker the control room pays better, since a union card now covers it.

**H)** Redeployment works for some and not for others, which is where the programs come in. Terminals in the United States, Britain and Australia run bridges with community colleges: up to eighteen months of paid training in electrical systems, hydraulics and network basics, funded by a small fee on every container. Graduation rates are good and placement rates honest, near three in four. The failures cluster. Workers past fifty, whose bodies were the reason for the change, often refuse to watch monitors, and pay scales earned through long service at the sharp end translate poorly into a grade for operators.

**I)** Safety argues both ways. Injuries that have to be reported at driverless berths have fallen by about half, and no one has died beneath a machine that could not see them, an event that once defined the job's worst days. The new work carries its own ledger. Operators sit for six-hour stretches under software that times every move and flags a pause, and researchers in three countries report eye strain, back pain and a tiredness from waiting for a fault that may not appear for an hour.

**J)** The pace is set by politics as much as by price. A terminal usually stands on public land, leased by an authority that answers to a government, and where labor is organized and expensive, machines arrive slowly and after a contract. Where a country never assembled a large docks workforce, the calculus flips: a new deep-water berth in a low-wage economy can be ordered without anyone to retrain and without a pension obligation to carry, which is why several operators there are skipping straight to a screen. Analysts call that jumping a rung.

**K)** Nobody in this business believes the story ends with cranes. The bottleneck has already moved: to the software that decides which box goes where, and to the few people who can debug a terminal at three in the morning. Whoever trains them will matter as much as whoever owns the land. Delgado's son has signed on for the electrical apprenticeship. Asked what he would tell young workers training for a quay they may never stand on, he said only: "Tell them the box still has to be caught. It is just that fewer of us have to be there to do it."

#### Section A 选词填空（Q26–35）

**词库词性分类**
- 形容词（4）：A) annual　C) comparable　M) considerate　N) unconditional
- 副词（3）：D) considerably　F) likewise　L) roughly
- 名词（4）：B) charges　H) receipts　J) savings　K) security
- 动词（4，形式互斥）：E) neglecting（-ing）　G) offsets（三单）　I) replace（原形）　O) delayed（过去分词/过去式）
- 答案：26 A、27 G、28 L、29 K、30 I、31 B、32 O、33 E、34 D、35 N（动4/名2/形2/副2）
- 干扰 5 词：C) comparable、F) likewise、H) receipts、J) savings、M) considerate（与答案词同词性；considerate/comparable 与形容词答案同槽竞争，savings/receipts 与名词答案同域易混，likewise 为纯副词形近干扰）

**26. A) annual**　语法依据：`the ___26___ visit to the vet`，定冠词 + 形容词 + 单数可数名词，需定语形容词。判定证据：并列清单"the annual visit to the vet, a heated bed, food prescribed..."列举的是养猫的固定开支项，annual（每年的例行项）与"清单式记账"主题唯一契合。干扰 M) considerate（"体贴的"只修饰有生命主体，✗ considerate visit）；C) comparable（需比较对象，下文无）；N) unconditional（"无条件的"不修饰一次兽医就诊）。

**27. G) offsets**　语法依据：`What he gives me ___27___ what he costs me`，主语为 what 引导的单数从句，需三单及物动词现在时（与前句现在完成时 I have done the sum 同框架）。判定证据：offset（抵消）扣住"两本账/收支对冲"的会计隐喻。干扰 I) replace（原形，三单主语下主谓不一致）；O) delayed（过去时与引语现在时冲突，且"给予"不能"推迟""花费"）；E) neglecting（须有 be 动词，且无宾语位置）。

**28. L) roughly**　语法依据：`has grown by ___28___ forty percent`，介词 by 后为"约数副词 + 数词"结构，空格紧贴 forty percent，只能填表约数的副词。干扰 D) considerably（只能修饰比较级，✗ considerably forty percent）；F) likewise（"同样地"不表数量）。

**29. K) security**　语法依据：`sells ___29___ rather than affection`，及物动词后需不可数/抽象名词，并与 affection（情感）构成"功能 vs 情感"对立。判定证据：冒号后 dental plans、physiotherapy、subscriptions covering emergency surgery 全是"防病于未然"的保障型产品，指向 security（保障）。干扰 J) savings（"省钱"与上文 spending grown by forty percent 的膨胀语境相反，且冒号清单无一为折扣）；H) receipts（票据，与"情感"不构成同一层面的对立）；B) charges（已被 31 的"put on a credit card"框架锁定）。

**30. I) replace**　语法依据：`the work no application has managed to ___30___`，manage to 后接动词原形，词库唯一原形动词。语义：治疗犬半天完成的工作，没有任何应用能取代。干扰 G) offsets（三单，不合 to 后）；O) delayed（分词/过去式）；E) neglecting（-ing 形式）。

**31. B) charges**　语法依据：`among the first ___31___ families put on a credit card`，among the first 后需复数名词，且须是"可被刷到信用卡上"的款项。判定证据：put on a credit card 的唯一支配对象是应付款项，charges（费用）成立。干扰 H) receipts（收据是付款凭证，不能被"刷上卡"）；J) savings（储蓄非"支出款项"，与"刷卡"矛盾）。

**32. O) delayed**　语法依据：`with elective surgery ___32___ until an installment plan arrives`，with + 名词 + 过去分词的独立结构，手术是"被推迟"的受事，且 until 表延续到分期到位，需表"延后"的过去分词。干扰 E) neglecting（with + 名词 + -ing 要求该名词为施事，surgery 不能发出"忽视"动作，且 neglect 需宾语）；G) offsets（三单，结构不允许）。

**33. E) neglecting**　语法依据：`while quietly ___33___ their own health`，while 后接及物动词 -ing 形式并带宾语 their own health，词库唯一 -ing 动词。语义与主句"keep a pet"构成"顾宠物、不顾自己"对照。干扰 I) replace（原形，不能立于 while 后且语义相反）；O) delayed（表被动须有 be，且 while + 过去分词无施读者）。

**34. D) considerably**　语法依据：`reported ___34___ less loneliness than those who did not`，副词修饰比较级 less，且为两组一次性对照（than），需程度副词。干扰 L) roughly（修饰约数，✗ roughly less）；F) likewise（"同样地"不能修饰比较级）。

**35. N) unconditional**　语法依据：`___35___ company`，名词 company（陪伴）前需定语形容词。判定证据：同位语 given freely and asking nothing except the door she comes through 把语义钉在"无条件"上。干扰 M) considerate（"体贴的"修饰人，✗ considerate company）；C) comparable（无比较基准）；A) annual（若填 26 已用，且"每年的陪伴"与"不计回报"无关）。
