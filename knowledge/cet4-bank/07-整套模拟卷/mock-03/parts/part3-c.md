#### Passage One

**Questions 46 to 50 are based on the following passage.**

Every day, millions of people stand in line: at bus stops, security checks, food counters. Few enjoy it, but most accept it. Yet queues raise a question that researchers have studied for decades: when does waiting feel fair? The answer, their work suggests, has less to do with minutes than with rules.

In one set of studies, psychologists watched customers at banks and stores. Long waits were accepted when the line moved steadily and the next person served had clearly arrived first. What upset people was a break in that order: a late customer served early, a counter closing without a word, or a worker helping someone they know. The rule "first come, first served" feels fair because it promises that waiting will not be wasted.

The same research separates two kinds of waiting. Occupied time, spent reading a notice or watching the line move, passes quickly and is judged short. Unoccupied time feels much longer, sometimes twice as long, even when the clock says otherwise. Empty waiting is where unfairness grows: with nothing to do, people start to compare, to count, and to look for blame. This explains why a crowded waiting room with a television can feel easier than a quiet, empty one.

The findings have practical effects. Hospitals do not treat patients in arrival order; they sort by danger, and most people accept this when the reason is explained. Airlines and call centers have learned that an honest wait time answers complaints better than a small speed-up does. A posted number gives the waiting person what the studies keep finding: a reason. Where no reason is offered, anger is, and it often targets the staff, who did not design the line.

Taken together, the research points to a simple lesson. Waiting feels fair when it follows a clear rule, when the rule is explained, and when everyone shares the cost. Minutes matter, but they are not the main event. The pain of a queue is mostly the fear of being cheated or ignored. Fair waiting, in the end, is a matter of respect.
