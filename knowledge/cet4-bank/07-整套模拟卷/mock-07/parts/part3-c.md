#### Passage One

**Questions 46 to 50 are based on the following passage.**

A cup of coffee in a rich city costs an hour of local wages. The farmer who picked the beans receives a few cents. That gap is usually argued about as a matter of fairness. Economists who trace prices along the chain tell a different story. The trouble is not the size of the share alone: the farmer has no say in setting it.

Consider what happens at harvest. Coffee cherries must be processed within days or the crop is ruined, so a farmer cannot wait. Debts to a supplier and school fees fall due in the same season. In many districts only two or three buyers travel the mountain roads, and they know one another. Most growers hear one price, from one truck, with no way to check it.

Selling together changes some of that arithmetic. When two hundred farms bring their crop to one shed, they can fill a truck. They can hire a scale and send one person to talk to buyers. Studies of cooperatives in East Africa and Central America find that members earn more per kilogram than farmers outside them. The rise is smaller than the labels promise. Group selling costs something too: time to weigh and record, and weeks of waiting for payment. Where trust is broken, farmers return to the buyer who pays cash the same day.

Certificates are a second answer, with mixed evidence. A label promising a fair price helps when the market falls, because such schemes usually set a floor. Above that floor the floor does nothing. Inspection and paperwork cost money. The poorest growers rarely join, since a certificate demands records they cannot keep. A premium meant to reach them often lands on farmers who are already better off.

The cheapest fix is also the least romantic: a price on a phone. When farmers learn what the crop is really worth, that offer stops being the only number in the room. Yet information alone does not create power. A grower with a bad road and no storage still sells on someone else's terms. The plain point is that a coffee price is a bargaining result, not a moral one. It records how many people between the farm and the cup can afford to wait.

**第 3 段**　钱这件事，让那些等着看裁员的人吃了一惊。在对增设传菜机器人的正餐桌式餐馆的研究中，工资单上的人数几乎没变。变的是这些工时待在哪儿：工时从"端菜"里流走，回到了门口迎宾和烤架前面。菜上桌时更热了，餐桌翻得更快了，平均账单也涨了。经理们还报告了一件更难量化的事：那些给迟迟不到岗的人整晚打电话的夜晚，过去了。

**第 4 段**　真正花钱的地方，是按机器重新装修后厨。置碗架要摆在厨师伸手能够到的地方；过道得拓宽，否则一只满载的托盘在拐角遇上机器，总要有东西摔坏。一台机器也许一晚省下一小时人工，可一旦把维修、充电位这些都算进去，它一个月可能又要吃掉一天的工钱。在这笔算术算不平的地方，机器就成了一面很贵的架子。

**第 5 段**　夜里打烊之前，还有一个更不显眼的问题。一台在周六晚上卡住的机器，需要一个能把它弄好的人，而这个人比它替换掉的那名传菜员更贵。用工短缺并没有消失，它挪到了一个申请人更少的技能上。那些经营得好的店，对待机器就像对待一台烤箱：要排班、要清洁、要修。它当初是为了补员工名册上的一个洞才买回来的，最后它自己也留在名册上。
