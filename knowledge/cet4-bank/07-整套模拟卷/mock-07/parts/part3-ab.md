**One copy, many readers**

**A)** Ask four students what they paid for course materials last September and you hear prices that sound like bad jokes: ninety dollars, two hundred, three hundred fifty, and — after a rental discount — one hundred thirty. Federal trackers put the average full-time undergraduate near twelve hundred dollars a year for books and supplies, a figure that has outrun tuition since the 1980s. For years, the used-book trade quietly absorbed that shock. What happened to it, and to the older habit of sharing collections outright, is one of the quietest policy stories on campus.

**B)** For most of the twentieth century, an expensive academic title was also a durable one. A hardcover chemistry text might serve a department for eight or nine years before a rival edition displaced it, and that unhurried pace gave secondhand trade room to work: bookstores bought back copies each May and resold them the following August, moving cost gently from one class to the next. The arrangement collapsed when publishers learned that revenue is made on new editions, not good ones. Revisions that once waited a decade now arrive on a three-year clock, and a book whose numbering changes before its science does becomes nearly dead the moment a professor assigns the latest printing.

**C)** The second blow fell on the packaging. Since the early 2000s most science and business texts have shipped sealed beside a one-time access code that unlocks homework, quizzes and lab data. Registration is what students actually need, and an old copy cannot supply it, so the market for bundled editions withered into scrap paper. A different trade filled the gap: campus stores now rent the twelve most requested books and send them out again each August. The rental rack turns a course book into a service, and the student who rents never plans to sell anything.

**D)** Publishers, losing money to the racks, adopted a strategy that makes the secondhand market irrelevant altogether. Under so-called inclusive-access programs, adopted by thousands of campuses, the price of a course's digital materials is folded into tuition-style billing at registration. Every enrolled student receives access on the first day whether or not anyone shops for a book, and the charge sits below the printed edition because there is nothing to print, ship or shelve. Complaints about being billed for readings nobody opens have grown just as fast as the programs themselves.

**E)** Libraries, which have absorbed textbook pressure longer than any other campus office, keep an older instrument in constant use. The reserve shelf is its name: an instructor deposits two or three copies of a required title, the desk lends them for two or three hours at a time, and a single volume passes through hundreds of hands in one examination week. Librarians say the line at the desk lengthens whenever a list price climbs, and hourly lending is often the only reason a chapter is free to read at two in the morning.

**F)** The same instinct — that no one institution should buy what many can share — built interlibrary loan. Any serious library holds far more than it can house, and the honest way to serve a reader is to borrow rather than duplicate. A patron at a small college clicks on a title in a shared union catalog, and within days the volume arrives by courier from a partner hundreds of miles away; journal articles arrive as scanned files inside the hour. Consortia of research libraries, municipal systems and community colleges have made hundreds of separate collections behave like one enormous, searchable, movable shelf.

**G)** The arithmetic of sharing is counter-intuitive and rarely appreciated. Staff time, courier fees and catalog work make every loan cost several dollars to fulfill — yet borrowing beats buying whenever a title is needed once, since purchasing a monograph that will be opened twice in a decade wastes far more. Request records show that a large share of loans involve books nobody had asked for in years. Joint membership strengthens the case: libraries pay predictable dues instead of growing their own stacks, and shared warehouses keep little-read volumes off campus.

**H)** Digitization, one might expect, ended this bookkeeping; instead it invented a newer scarcity. When a library buys a printed book it owns that copy forever and may lend it without limit. When it acquires an electronic title it typically signs a license: the holding expires, must be renewed, and is priced by publishers far above what a reader pays in a store. Some publishers refuse to sell libraries popular books at any price, or cap how many copies may circulate, so a campus of thousands may face a two-hundred-day wait for one licensed copy of the novel assigned in first-year composition.

**I)** Scanning technology has quietly blurred the boundary between borrowing and copying. Overhead cradle scanners now turn a lent volume into usable text at a thousand pages an hour, and many university libraries run desks where a staff member extracts a requested chapter within a day, replacing the courier entirely. The file born on the reading-room machine can reach a registered class the same week. The result makes a single chapter as easy to obtain as an article, a practice the publishing industry watches closely.

**J)** Sharing systems reflect the hierarchies that build them. Rules written into every consortium cap what a member may request, so a small teaching college with a thin shelf may borrow only a set number of volumes a year however urgent the need, while the large research libraries that fill most orders guard their staff budgets from the workload. The institutions least able to buy depend most on generosity they cannot return in kind. Scholars who study loan records call the arrangement an unequal truce rather than a market.

**K)** A different answer has arrived from outside the market altogether. Open textbooks are complete, peer-reviewed introductions authored by faculty and released under licenses that permit anyone to read, adapt and redistribute them without paying a cent. What began as a curiosity among community-college instructors is becoming institutional policy: growing numbers of colleges advertise two-year pathways that carry no book bill at all, and state legislatures have funded grants to accelerate the shift. The evidence on grades is modest but encouraging; the evidence on wallets is overwhelming.

**26. F) hue**　语法依据：`pick the ___ of every wall, shelf edge and price tag`，the…of 框架需可数名词单数；"为每面墙、货架边缘、价签挑选____"只有"色调"义成立。干扰 K) route（"墙的路线"不通）、G) impulse（"挑选冲动"语义不立）。

**27. E) drift**　语法依据：`shoppers on a long visit ___ through its quiet corners`，复数主语后需谓语动词原形，与 through 连用表"漫无目的地穿行"。干扰 C) browsing（-ing 形不能独立作谓语）；N) triggers（三单形与复数主语不一致，主谓一致即排除）；L) steer（及物，"steer through corners"无宾语不成立）。

**28. A) ambient**　语法依据：`The ___ light of a store, that soft general glow around the goods, gets the same treatment`，同位语"环绕商品的柔和弥漫光"正是 ambient light（环境光）的术语定义。干扰 D) deliberate（"刻意的光"不等于均匀弥漫光）；O) urgent（urgent light 不成搭配，语义不立）。

**29. M) subtly**　语法依据：`it acts ___ , the way background music does`，acts 后需方式副词，"像背景音乐一样起作用"锁定"不易察觉地"。干扰 B) barely（"几乎不起作用"与下文"仍左右购物篮"直接矛盾）；H) increasingly（无时间递进语境，且与方式同位语 the way... does 不搭）；J) roughly（"粗略地起作用"不通）。

**30. B) barely**　语法依据：`___ recall a single one`，"a single one"极小量框架要求近乎否定的程度副词"几乎不"。干扰 J) roughly（"大约记起一个"与 yet 转折"目光仍在左右购物篮"的对比逻辑相悖）；H) increasingly recall（全篇无渐进线索）。

**31. K) route**　语法依据：`takes the shortest ___ from door to bread, not the winding loop`，与 loop（环线）构成"最短路线 vs 被引导绕行"对照，规划路径义只有 route 与 loop 同类。干扰 F) hue / G) impulse（语义不立）。

**32. I) negligible**　语法依据：`paint alone is ___ beside price and habit`，be＋beside（与……相比）需"微不足道"义形容词，呼应 skeptics 的削弱立场。干扰 D) deliberate / O) urgent（"是刻意的/是紧急的"均无法与"与价格习惯相比"构成让步义）。

**33. G) impulse**　语法依据：`the classic ___ buy`，名词作定语，impulse buy（冲动购买）为零售固定术语；"拿着六样清单离场时却有十一样"进一步锁定。

**34. C) browsing**　语法依据：`spend forty idle minutes ___ the home-goods aisles`，spend＋时间＋(in)＋-ing 框架，词库唯一 -ing 形；browse 可直接接场所作宾语。干扰 E) drift（原形不能进该框架，且需 through 等介词）。

**35. L) steer**　语法依据：`waits to ___ them deeper inside`，to 后需动词原形，且为"及物动词＋宾语＋方向补语"框架（steer sb + 方向），"把顾客引向深处"正是动线设计用语。干扰 E) drift（不及物，不能使动"drift them"）；N) triggers（三单形不能置于 to 后）。
