<summary>Passage One 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

In 1795 the French government offered a prize of twelve thousand francs to anyone who could supply armies with food that would not spoil on the march. A Parisian confectioner named Nicolas Appert took up the problem and spent fourteen years failing at it. His method, announced in 1810, was simple and workable: food sealed in glass bottles with cork and wax, then lowered into boiling water for a measured time. What he did not know, and what nobody could know for another fifty years, was why it worked, namely that heat was killing invisible life inside the jar and that the seal kept the rest out. Louis Pasteur's later experiments explained the part Appert had guessed at. Glass was awkward for soldiers, so two London publishers took out a patent for containers of iron plated with tin, and the metal can was born. Remarkably, the opener for it had to wait half a century. Instructions printed on early tins told the user to cut a hole with a bayonet or to drive in a chisel and a hammer, and collectors still pay heavily for cans that were never touched. By the American Civil War, factories were sealing tens of millions of tins a year, and for the first time a city family could eat summer peas in February.

Every prediction of the death of radio has been wrong, and the medium's latest comeback is the one nobody forecast. <summary>Passage Three 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

A tide clock on a harbor wall looks broken, because its single hand points to high water hours away from what the ordinary clock says. It is not broken. The ocean answers the moon, which rises about fifty minutes later each night, so a shoreline keeps a schedule that drifts against ours. Creatures living between the waves follow that same schedule. When biologists carried crabs from an estuary into a laboratory tank, far from any tide, and kept the room dark and quiet, the animals still scrambled toward the surface at the hours when their home beach would have been bare. Their calendar was built inside them. Human beings keep two clocks, not one. The master pacemaker sits deep in the brain, is reset by morning light, and holds us to a day slightly longer than twenty-four hours, which we correct at every dawn. But the liver, the heart and the pancreas run on timetables of their own, set less by light than by the hour we eat. That is why a night shift harms people more than lost sleep suggests: the brain has been moved onto a new clock while the rest of the body reads an old one. Researchers who looked for a lunar influence on human sleep found effects far weaker than in crabs, and mostly in communities without electric light. The tide clocks keep turning on the harbor wall, and the fishers who read them are the only people there obeying two calendars at once.
