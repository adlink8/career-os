<summary>Passage One 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

Every moving staircase in a shopping mall descends from a patent that was never built. In 1859 Nathan Ames described revolving stairs in the United States, a wheel of steps turning on a central hub, and the idea went nowhere because nobody could work out how a person should step on or off safely. Inclined lifts came next. In 1891 Jesse Reno carried passengers up a ramp at Coney Island on a moving surface of hinged cleats rather than separate steps, and a year later George Wheeler patented something closer to what riders know today. The word escalator itself arrived commercially. Charles Seeberger, who had bought patent rights from a rival, registered the name in 1899 and showed a machine at an exhibition in Paris; the elevator firm already famous for its safety brake later built the units that opened in the London Underground in 1911, the first in any subway. Seeberger kept no protection for his word, because the public used it for every device of the kind. Details hidden from riders remain: near each landing the steps flatten, so that a foot leaves a moving surface onto a level one, and the handrail is geared to run slightly faster than the steps, so that a sleeve cannot be drawn between the two.

Open the door of the freezer cut into a Norwegian mountain and you find boxes on steel shelves holding a copy of nearly everything farmers have grown for ten thousand years. The vault began work in 2008; tenants deposit their samples at minus eighteen degrees, cold enough for most crop seeds to last for decades. The purpose is not nostalgia. <summary>Passage Three 原文（Tapescript）</summary>

A hibernating ground squirrel looks like a corpse. Its body temperature falls from about thirty-eight degrees to a few above freezing, its heart slows from two hundred beats a minute to perhaps five, and breathing becomes nearly invisible. For a century biologists called this state deep sleep, and the label is wrong. Sleep is repaid: deprive an animal of it and the missed hours come back. Torpor is something else, a programmed lowering of the setting at which heat is held, and an animal taken through the cold months without any sleep loss shows no such debt. What still puzzles researchers is why these animals do not simply stay under. Individuals rouse every two or three weeks, burn through fat they cannot spare, and slide back down, spending as much as a quarter of the winter awake. Two explanations compete: chemistry that has to be cleared only at a normal temperature, and a defense system against illness that stops working in the cold. The animals also solve problems that cost patients dearly. Waste is reabsorbed rather than passed for months, and skeleton and muscle are kept that a body lying still for a season would give up. That is why one hospital protocol now cools cardiac-arrest patients to around fifteen degrees, buying an hour in which an operation can safely be done, and why donated parts travel farther when their chemistry is slowed first.
