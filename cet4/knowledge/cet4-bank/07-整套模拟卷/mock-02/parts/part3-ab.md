**The checkpoint that never subtracts**

**A)** At 5:40 on a Monday morning, Dana Whitfield joins a line of two hundred people outside a midwestern terminal and begins a ritual that would have been unrecognizable to travelers of 1990: belt out, laptop out, shoes in a bin, hands raised. Some 1.9 million passengers move through American checkpoints on a typical day, making this the largest recurring physical examination the country has staged in peacetime. Nearly everyone accepts it. Nearly no one can say what it prevents, and that gap is where the argument lives.

**B)** The enterprise is hard to picture. Since the federal government took over bag and body screening in late 2001, the United States has spent more than a hundred billion dollars on the task, hired some fifty thousand officers, and bought machines whose maintenance contracts could endow a small college. Complaints still arrive by the tens of thousands each year, and the agency's service line handles far more calls about confiscated sunscreen than about stopped attacks. Passengers know the cost in hours and dignity; analysts know it in dollars.

**C)** Critics call parts of this theater, and they are not entirely wrong. The measures that shape the checkpoint most visibly, the limit on liquids, the bare feet, the tall scanner, were each adopted in the days after an attempted attack and kept long after that technique had been exhausted. A rule answering yesterday's headline carries a real political advantage: after a failure the public demands that something change, and a new visible rule proves that it did. A rule chosen for expected harm avoided would be harder to defend at a press conference.

**D)** Considered as arithmetic, the trade looks strange. Aviation security events kill fewer Americans in a year than a bad ice storm kills at a few busy terminals, and the car journey to the airport is deadlier per mile than the flight itself; even the queue carries a cardiac toll. One often-cited estimate divided the program's annual cost by the deaths it plausibly prevented and produced a figure many times larger than what official agencies agree to pay for a statistical life. The checkpoint survives every audit, because its ledger is written in fear rather than in fatalities.

**E)** The defense of screening does not rest on arithmetic, and its advocates are right that a single ratio misses the point. Hardening a target changes what attackers attempt, not only whether one attempt succeeds. Two disasters, a bomb hidden in a radio over the Atlantic in 1985 and four planes turned into weapons sixteen years later, began inside gaps that later measures closed. Since then the country has piled watch-lists, federal screeners, reinforced cockpit doors, armed marshals and risk-based vetting into a structure engineers compare to swiss cheese: the claim is not that every hole is filled, but that the holes no longer line up.

**F)** That piling-up shows sedimentation rather than strategy. The liquids limit of 2006 followed a plot discovered in Britain. The requirement to remove shoes grew out of a Christmas morning attempt two years earlier. The body scanners arrived after one unsuccessful effort on a flight bound for Detroit in 2009. None of the three has been weighed against a cheaper substitute, and no office keeps a list of rules that have outlived the reason for them. Deleting a measure would require someone to certify that the country is now slightly less protected, which is not a career move.

**G)** Other countries spend comparable money on different assumptions. Israel examines far fewer travelers for far longer, working through interviewers who ask questions and read the answers rather than through machines that read densities. The United States runs the same logic in reverse: Trusted Traveler programs clear low-risk passengers quickly on the strength of a background check and a fee, so that scarce attention can go to the unfamiliar face. Both approaches treat a person's history as more informative than that person's shoes. The federal scheme that trained officers to spot nervousness was reviewed and found to have no validated link between the cues taught and the contraband sought.

**H)** The costs fall unevenly. Black travelers, Muslim travelers and people with visible disabilities report secondary screening at rates no threat statistic explains, and the pat-down that follows a false alarm is remembered far longer than the flight that was never endangered. Parents of children with metal implants and passengers with pacemakers describe a system with no quick way to say that they belong here. A regime that cannot explain why it stopped this traveler and not the one behind will keep reproducing its errors and call them caution.

**I)** Then there is the question of what the queue is counted against. Every officer unbending a paper clip is an officer not watching cargo, borders, or the likelier insider with a key card. Analysts of attacks on crowded transport note that the least protected point in a journey is not the departure lounge but the parking structure, the shuttle bus and the platform where people wait shoulder to shoulder for the train toward the terminal. Protection that followed the traveler past the checkpoint would be cheaper in some places and uncomfortable in others.

**J)** What, then, is a fair verdict? Aviation security is judged against a baseline no one can see: the attempts that never started because an attacker expected a wall. Historians note that hijackings, which ran at a frightening rate through the late 1960s and 1970s, were engineered away by an unspectacular bundle of steps, no smoking in the cabin, screening of carry-on bags, locked cockpit doors. The disappearance was so complete that today's passengers cannot reconstruct the danger that vanished. That is the strongest case for the checkpoint, and also the weakest kind of proof, because prevention done well is indistinguishable from nothing happening.

**K)** The coming decade may quietly delete the parts of this scene that passengers resent most. Machines now entering service reconstruct the contents of a bag in three dimensions, letting liquids and laptops stay where they are; several European airports have published deadlines for the change. Corridors that recognize a face instead of a boarding pass are being tested on three continents, promising a line that never fully stops. Whether these inventions reduce harm or only reduce grumbling is the question on which billions will be spent or saved.

**防撞题说明**：本题主题"食物浪费与保质期行为"与 `06-全真阅读模拟器/week3/Day21`（Section C 仔细阅读，Use By 与 Best Before 两类标签之辨、风险厌恶心理、立法统一标签）在**板块层级与论证重心上完全分离**：本篇 260 词选词短文只把日期印字当作一个背景触发点（28 空），主体论域是家庭丢弃行为的成因（大包装、冰箱视野盲区、份量）与可测量的行为干预（清单、冷冻、小餐盘、瑕疵果蔬降价），不复现 Day 21 的双标签二分法、五折实验与监管统一主张。

**26. G) little**　语法依据：`is now done with ___ thought at all about`，介词 with 后接"限定词 + 名词 thought"，且句末 at all 为否定极项，要求前面是一个具否定意义的限定词——little（几乎没有）唯一成立。干扰 A) absolutely（须修饰 no thought，absolutely thought 不合法）；L) regularly / M) roughly（频度/约数副词不能限定抽象名词 thought）；B) abundant（"with abundant thought at all" 与 at all 的否定极性冲突）。

**27. D) edible**　语法依据：`was still perfectly ___ and could have been cooked that evening`，系动词 was 后需表语形容词，副词 perfectly 修饰它；and 后"当晚本可下锅"把词义钉死在"还能吃"。干扰 B) abundant（"数量丰富"与"本可烹食"无逻辑关系，且主语是已被丢弃的食物本身，谈不上丰富）；H) modest（"适度"不能形容食物可食性）。

**28. C) date**　语法依据：`before the printed ___ arrives`，the printed + 单数可数名词，谓语 arrives 要求一个"会到期"的时间性名词——printed date（印刷日期）唯一。干扰 J) portion（份量不会"arrives"到期）；N) surplus（过剩量无到期义，且 surplus 常接 of 而非 printed 定语）；K) reduction（语义不合）。

**29. M) roughly**　语法依据：`was thrown out ___ two weeks before any change ... would have appeared`，副词修饰数量短语 two weeks（大约两周），与调查自述的模糊性一致。干扰 L) regularly（频度副词不能修饰 two weeks 这一度量短语，且主句为具体一次性的 had been binned 事件，与习惯性副词冲突）；A) absolutely（absolutely 不修饰裸数词短语，absolutely two weeks before 不成立）；G) little（已被 26 的否定极项框架锁定，且 ✗ little two weeks）。

**30. I) plan**　语法依据：`Households can ___ a week of meals in ten minutes`，情态动词 can 后接动词原形并带"一周伙食"这一受事宾语，ten minutes 说明这是"事先安排"动作——plan a week of meals 唯一。干扰 E) kept（过去分词/过去式，不能跟在 can 后）；F) labeling / O) writing（-ing 形式，词法不合 can 后原形要求）；且本空语义上不存在"标注/书写一周伙食"。

**31. O) writing**　语法依据：`Freezing bread ... and ___ the day and month on the container in marker make leftovers visible`，与前面动名词 Freezing 并列作复合主语（谓语 make 为复数），故需 -ing 形式；宾语是 the day and month（日子），in marker（用记号笔）说明是"书写"。干扰 F) labeling（label 的宾语应是被贴标的容器，不能说 label the day and month，搭配错）；E) kept（"be kept" 与 in marker 不搭，且与 Freezing 不并列）；I) plan（原形，无法作并列主语）。

**32. K) reduction**　语法依据：`recorded a sharp ___ in what came back uneaten`，a sharp + 单数可数名词 + in 表"在某方面下降"，recorded 是研究数据动词——a sharp reduction in 唯一。干扰 N) surplus（surplus 搭配 of 不搭 in，且"过剩量下降"不能由餐盘实验"记录到"）；J) portion（"a sharp portion in" 不成结构）；B) abundant / D) edible / H) modest（形容词，词性不合）。

**33. J) portion**　语法依据：`leave the store with a ___ sized for the household rather than for the month`，a + 单数可数名词，后接 sized for 过去分词短语作定语，语义为"按家庭人口而非按月计的份量"。干扰 N) surplus（"买回一份额外的过剩量 sized for the household"自相矛盾，surplus 本身即"用不完之量"，不会被裁量到合适）；C) date（已被 28 的 printed + arrives 框架锁定）；K) reduction（"带着一个减缩"语义不通）。

**34. E) kept**　语法依据：`Freshness can be ___ for weeks at zero degrees`，can be + 过去分词构成被动，主语 freshness（新鲜度）是受事，for weeks 要求"保存"义——kept 唯一。干扰 I) plan（新鲜度不能被"计划"数周）；F) labeling / O) writing（-ing 形式不能置于 can be 之后构成被动；be writing 的进行解读要求主语为有生命施事）。

**35. H) modest**　语法依据：`The gains are ___ but real: ... filled their bins a fifth less`，but 构成让步对比，"___ 但真实"要求前项是"不大"义的褒贬中性形容词，冒号后"少五分之一"印证"幅度有限"——modest but real 为固定让步搭配。干扰 B) abundant（"丰富但真实"无对比逻辑，且与 a fifth less 的小幅数据冲突）；D) edible（不能修饰 gains）；G) little（little 作表语形容 gains 不成立，现代英语中该让步框架只用 modest/small）。
